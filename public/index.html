A probabilistic model of Lula's chance in the October 25 second round against Flávio Bolsonaro, aggregating the public valid-vote polls and simulating 40,000 elections. Move the sliders to test different assumptions about polling error and late swing.

This is a model, not a prediction. The race is a statistical tie; polls can be collectively wrong, as Brazil's 2022 runoff showed. The output is a range of plausible outcomes under assumptions you control — not a forecast of who will win.

How the model works

The model treats the runoff as a two-way race on valid votes and asks: given the polls, what range of outcomes is plausible once we admit that polls can be wrong together?

1 · Aggregate

Each poll's Lula valid-vote share is pooled into a weighted average. Weight = recency decay × (optional) √sample size, capped at 5,000; every pollster is treated as equally accurate. By default only each pollster's latest poll counts, so a firm that publishes every few days doesn't get extra say, and polls released more than 10 days before the vote are left out. Where a poll reported only total votes, the valid share is computed as Lula ÷ (Lula + Flávio).

2 · Model the uncertainty

The headline number isn't the point — the spread is. Each of 40,000 simulated elections draws a final margin from a normal distribution centered on the weighted average (plus any swing adjustment), with standard deviation set by the polling error slider. This "all polls off together" term dominates because pooled sampling error is negligible.

3 · Count

Lula's win probability is simply the share of the 40,000 simulations in which his share exceeds 50%. The distribution chart is the histogram of those draws.

Honest limits

  • Garbage in, garbage out: if the polls share a systematic bias, so will this.
  • No state-level or electoral-geography modeling — it's a national popular-vote runoff, so one number decides it.
  • Palver, Instituto França and Brazil Economy/Colectta released eve-of-vote polls, but their figures weren't found in any source, so they're not in the model.
  • Pollsters that release only total votes or use different question wording aren't adjusted for house effects.
  • Normality is an approximation; real polling errors can be fat-tailed.
